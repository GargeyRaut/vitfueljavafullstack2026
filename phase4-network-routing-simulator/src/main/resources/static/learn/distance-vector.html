Level 4 — Routing

Distance Vector Routing

How real routers converge on shortest paths without any single one of them ever seeing the whole network.

1 · Overview

Dijkstra and Bellman-Ford both assume something no real router actually has: a complete view of the whole graph. Distance vector routing is how routing actually works in protocols like RIP — each router only knows two things: the cost to its direct neighbors, and whatever those neighbors currently claim about everyone else. By repeatedly gossiping these claims, the whole network's tables gradually converge to the true shortest distances — without a single router ever seeing the full map.

2 · Why it exists

Giving every router a full copy of the network graph (as Dijkstra/Bellman-Ford require) doesn't scale, and doesn't match reality — routers only physically connect to a few neighbors. Distance vector routing solves this with only local information and local communication, at the cost of slower convergence and some correctness edge-cases (like "counting to infinity" after certain failures) that link-state protocols were later designed to avoid.

3 · Real-world analogy

It's office gossip about who's fastest at a task. You don't personally know everyone in the company — but your deskmate tells you "I heard Priya can do it in 3 days", and if you know it takes you 1 day to relay a request to your deskmate, you now know "via my deskmate, Priya is 4 days away" — without ever meeting Priya. Everyone keeps updating their own belief this way until the gossip settles on the true fastest route to everyone.

4 · Technical explanation

1
Each router starts knowing only itself, at cost 0.
2
Each round, every router sends its entire current vector (destination → cost) to each direct neighbor.
3
Each router receiving a neighbor's vector adds the cost of that link to every entry in it, and keeps whichever is cheaper: what it already knew, or this new via-neighbor option.
4
Repeat every round. Once a full round changes nothing anywhere, the network has converged.

6 · Step-by-step worked example (narrated)

1
Round 1: every router learns its direct neighbors' costs — that's as far as one round of gossip can reach.
2
Round 2: routers now also learn about their neighbors' neighbors, because that information arrived in round 1's gossip and gets relayed onward.
3
Each further round extends everyone's knowledge one more "hop" of gossip outward, and corrects any entry where a cheaper path was just discovered.
4
Once nothing changes in a full round, run the same demo on the Dijkstra lesson and compare — the final distances from A should match exactly.

7 · Common mistakes

Assuming convergence is instant. Information has to physically propagate hop-by-hop through gossip rounds — a distant router can take several rounds to learn about a far-away change.
Forgetting routers only know cost, not the actual path. A distance-vector router knows "reach X for cost 7 via neighbor Y" — it does not know the full route beyond that one next hop, unlike link-state protocols that build a complete map.
Not accounting for "counting to infinity". When a link fails, distance-vector protocols can briefly and repeatedly propagate stale, gradually-worsening estimates back and forth before converging — a well-known failure mode that real protocols like RIP add special-case rules to limit.
